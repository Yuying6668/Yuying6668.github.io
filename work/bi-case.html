From ERP data to a sales dashboard a sales manager actually uses

A full BI build for a multi-market jewellery retailer: from asking the right questions, to modelling the data, to an automated data warehouse, to an interactive Power BI report.

Timeline
Jul 2025 – Feb 2026
Context
BI training case
User
Sales manager
Tools
Power BI · SQL · ETL

01The brief

The sales manager needed one consistent view of sales amount, sales quantity and growth, sliced by country, product, business line and time, in different currencies. The raw data sat in the company's ERP system.

02Requirements → model

Before opening Power BI, I turned the request into a data model with a simple "2W1H" method:

WhoSales manager: the primary user and decision maker
WhatSales amount, quantity, growth rate → measures, grouped into fact tables
HowBy time, country, product, business line, currency → attributes, grouped into dimension tables

A bus matrix mapped every fact to its dimensions and showed which were conformed (shared across facts). That became a star schema, designed first as a conceptual and then as a logical model.

03The data warehouse

One dashboard is easy; a reliable data layer that scales to the whole organisation is the real work. I designed an enterprise data warehouse in four layers:

StagingRaw extracts from the ERP (customers, products, sales transactions)
DimensionsConformed Customer and Product dimensions, with SCD Type 1 for customers and SCD Type 2 to keep product history
FactsSales transactions, loaded incrementally via a load-metadata table, so only new records are processed
Data martsCurated views and pre-aggregated facts for the sales and marketing domains
1 · ExtractERP → staging
2 · DimensionsLoad with SCD logic
3 · FactsIncremental load
4 · RefreshPower BI semantic model

Stored procedures handle each step, and a master package runs them in order (dimensions always before facts) inside atomic transactions to protect data integrity.

04The report

In Power BI I cleaned and shaped the data in Power Query, set up relationships in the model view and built the report:

  • Overview: KPI cards (average selling price, quantity, total sales, YoY %) and breakdowns by country, subcategory, month and business line.
  • Time intelligence: trends and growth over time.
  • Sales detail: drill-down for deeper questions.
  • Currency slicer so markets can be compared in one currency (e.g. DKK).

05What I took from it

Good BI starts with the questions, not the charts. Defining who needs what, and how they want to slice it, made the model, the warehouse and the report fall into place, and makes the next dashboard much faster to build on the same foundation.
